Before you start: a letter to you

I know how this feels. You’ve sat this exam before, you’ve read the slides, you’ve maybe even understood them while reading — and then in the exam room the page stayed half-empty. That isn’t because you can’t do this. It’s because nobody showed you how the pieces fit together. Facts you don’t understand slide off the brain in a week. Ideas you understand stay.

So this guide doesn’t hand you facts. It tells you one story, from the first page to the last, and every topic on both exams is a chapter of that story. If you follow the story, you won’t need to memorise most of this — you’ll be able to rebuild it in the exam room, because it will make sense.

Please read it in order. Every chapter leans on the one before. And please, do the little “your turn” exercises with a pen. You don’t learn to swim by reading about water.

You can pass these. Let’s go.

The story in one page

Here is a question that sounds silly but is the heart of computer science:

What can a machine do if it can only remember so much?

We’ll build machines, starting from the weakest imaginable and giving them a little more power each time — and at every step we’ll ask: what can it do now that it couldn’t before, and what still defeats it?

And now the twist that ties your two courses together: a programming language is built out of exactly these machines. When you press “run”, your code — which is just a long string of characters — goes through:

  1. a lexer, which is a finite automaton (Ch. 2), chopping text into words;
  2. a parser, which is a grammar with a stack (Ch. 7–10), finding the structure;
  3. an interpreter, which walks that structure and makes it happen (Ch. 22) — keeping track of names, scopes, function calls and parameters (Ch. 19–20).

Then we zoom out: how do humans keep huge programs understandable? With abstraction — hiding details behind clean walls (Ch. 21). And finally we meet a completely different way to think about programs: functional programming in Haskell, where there are no variables that change and no loops, only definitions (Ch. 15–18) — plus a glimpse of logic programming and concurrency (Ch. 23–24).

How to read the boxes

Intuition — the idea in plain words; read these twice. Definition — the precise version you write on the exam. Recipe — a step-by-step method you can follow under stress. Trap — where students lose points. Exam — what the examiner wants to see, often a full model answer. Grey dashed boxes are your turn — do them before reading the answer.

What the exams look like — so you know what we’re aiming at

PL Programspråk 5DV086 — 60 points, 36 to pass

Every past exam you have follows the same shape, and it’s a friendly one:

  • ~20 points on grammars: build a context-free grammar, or convert one to Chomsky normal form, or run CYK. (Ch. 7, 10)
  • ~20 points of “explain”: precedence & associativity, the parts of an interpreter, encapsulation & information hiding, pass-by-value vs reference, variables in functional languages. (Ch. 8, 19–22)
  • ~20 points of Haskell: write myRev, compress/decompress, a fold, a list function. (Ch. 15–18)

Some questions appeared word for word on two exams in a row. That’s good news: this exam rewards preparation.

FCS Datavetenskapens grunder 5DV037 — 50 points, 25 to pass

Ten questions in four hours: true/false, closure proofs, a table of language classes, a pumping-lemma proof, CNF + CYK, building a Turing machine, and three hard ☆ questions worth only one point each (do them last). You get a sheet with the pumping lemmas and the formal definitions — so you need to use them, not memorise them.
