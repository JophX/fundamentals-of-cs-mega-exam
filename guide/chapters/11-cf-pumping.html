11. Pumping lemma for CFLs, and closure FCS

Intuition

In a very tall parse tree, some variable must repeat on one path (finitely many variables — pigeonhole again). The subtree between the two occurrences can be repeated or removed. That pumps two pieces \(u\) and \(v\) at the same time (one on each side of the middle \(y\)). This is why CFLs can match two counts (\(a^nb^n\)) but not three (\(a^nb^nc^n\)).

The lemma (exam appendix)

If \(L\) is (infinite) context-free, there is \(n\) such that every \(w\in L\) with \(|w|\ge n\) can be written \(w=xuyvz\) with \(|uyv|\le n\), \(|uv|\ge1\), such that \(xu^iyv^iz\in L\) for all \(i\ge0\).

Recipe: proving L is NOT context-free
  1. Assume \(L\) is CF with constant \(n\).
  2. Choose \(w\in L\), \(|w|\ge n\), depending on \(n\) — usually blocks of length \(n\): \(a^nb^nc^n\).
  3. The window \(uyv\) has length ≤ \(n\), so it can touch at most two neighbouring blocks — but it can be anywhere (unlike the regular lemma). List the cases by which blocks \(u\) and \(v\) touch.
  4. In each case choose \(i\) (0 or 2) that breaks membership.
  5. All cases lead to contradiction ⇒ not CF.

Example 1: \(\{a^kb^kc^k\}\)

Assume CF, constant \(n\). \(w=a^nb^nc^n\). Since \(|uyv|\le n\), \(uv\) can’t contain both \(a\)’s and \(c\)’s (the b-block of length \(n\) is between them). Case 1: \(uv\) has no \(c\). Then pumping \(i=0\) removes at least one \(a\) or \(b\) but no \(c\): the counts are no longer equal. Case 2: \(uv\) has no \(a\): symmetric, \(i=0\) leaves \(n\) a’s and fewer b’s or c’s. (Also if \(u\) or \(v\) contains two different letters, \(i=2\) puts letters out of order.) Contradiction — not CF.

Example 2: \(\{ww\mid w\in\{a,b\}^*\}\)

\(w=a^nb^na^nb^n\). The window touches at most two adjacent blocks. \(i=0\): if the window is in the first half, the first half loses letters but the second doesn’t; the string (if even length) is no longer of the form \(ww\) because its middle shifts into the \(b\)-blocks mismatching. Case analysis (first half / middle / second half) gives a contradiction each time. Not CF. (Note: \(\{ww^R\}\) is CF — mirror, not copy.)

Example 3: \(\{a^{k}\mid k \text{ prime}\}\), \(\{a^{k^2}\}\)

Over one letter, CF = regular, so these are not CF either (same proofs as Ch. 6 with \(|uv|\) in place of \(|y|\)).

FCS exam Q4 (2022/2025 cover): the language printed is actually context-free

\(L=\{w\mid |w|_a>2|w|_b\}\cup\{w\mid 2|w|_a<|w|_b\}\). Each part is recognised by a PDA that uses its stack as a counter (add 1 per \(a\), subtract 2 per \(b\); accept if the counter is positive), and CFLs are closed under union. So \(L\) is context-free, and no correct proof of the opposite exists. A grammar for it:

\(S\to A\mid B,\ A\to EaE\mid EaA,\ B\to FbF\mid FbB,\) \(E\to aEaEbE\mid aEbEaE\mid bEaEaE\mid\lambda,\) \(F\to bFbFaF\mid bFaFbF\mid aFbFbF\mid\lambda\)

If this happens on your exam: don’t freeze. Write briefly why the attempt fails, give the PDA/union argument, and say you believe the question contains a mistake — then move on. If a similar question appears with three letters, e.g. \(\{w\in\{a,b,c\}^*\mid |w|_a=|w|_b=|w|_c\}\), it is non-CF: intersect with the regular \(a^*b^*c^*\) to get \(a^nb^nc^n\) (CFL ∩ regular is CF, so if \(L\) were CF, \(a^nb^nc^n\) would be too).
